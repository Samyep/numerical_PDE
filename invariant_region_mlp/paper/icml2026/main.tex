\begin{abstract}
Multilevel Picard (MLP) methods repeatedly feed finite-sample value--gradient estimates into a nonlinear generator. We study a simple failure mode: a noisy child state can leave a region that PDE structure certifies for the exact solution, and the generator can amplify that infeasible component before Monte Carlo averaging has a chance to cancel it. We propose constraint-aware MLP, which retracts child states into certified feasible sets immediately before nonlinear reuse, with both samplewise nearest-point and batch-coupled gauge realizations. On a known high-dimensional MLP counterexample, a certified active subspace makes the offending generator vanish; recursively enforcing that subspace removes every nonlinear correction pathwise and yields exact full-state MSE $(4-2/\pi)/N$, independent of ambient dimension. In a paired 100--160D HJB study, Batch-IR reduces value error by 57--62\% relative to Samplewise IR, with gains also persisting in deeper nonlinear-funding recursions. Matched generator and level-correction diagnostics, thresholded-driver ties, and inactive-constraint controls delimit this finite-budget advantage rather than establish universal dominance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
High-dimensional MLP methods combine Picard iteration with Monte Carlo and multilevel differences to solve semilinear parabolic PDEs without spatial grids \citep{E2021,HutzenthalerKruse2020,NeufeldWu2025}. Their recursive structure creates a numerical issue that is easy to miss: a finite-sample value--gradient state is itself an input to later nonlinear evaluations. Thus an error that would be harmless under linear averaging can become large after a norm, square, threshold, or other nonlinear transformation.

Suppose PDE structure certifies that the exact state $Y^\star=(u,z)$ lies in a set $\mathcal C(t,x)$, but a Monte Carlo child $\widehat Y$ lies outside. The usual recursion evaluates $f(t,x,\widehat Y)$ anyway. Our proposal is minimal:
\begin{equation}
\widehat Y \;\longrightarrow\; \widetilde Y=\mathcal R(\widehat Y)\in\mathcal C(t,x)
\;\longrightarrow\; f(t,x,\widetilde Y),
\label{eq:principle}
\end{equation}
where the correction occurs \emph{inside} the tree, before nonlinear reuse. We call this constraint-aware MLP.

We study two realizations. \textbf{Samplewise IR-MLP} applies the metric projection independently to each child. \textbf{Batch-IR} uses one common gauge factor for a sibling Monte Carlo batch. Samplewise projection has cleaner geometry; batchwise contraction couples samples and moves the empirical mean, but can suppress batch spread more aggressively.

Clipping inside MLP is already used in truncated MLP and SCaSML \citep{Beck2020,Fan2026}. Our contribution is not the projection primitive or its insertion before a driver call. It is the connection between \emph{PDE-certified value--gradient geometry}, the nonlinear feedback it removes, and the statistical choice of a separable or batch-coupled correction (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

The main theoretical result is an exact structural rescue of the dimensionality counterexample of \citet{HutzenthalerNguyen2025}. The true gradient lies in $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$, on which the offending driver vanishes. Enforcing this structure recursively removes every nonlinear correction pathwise. Ordinary samplewise convergence is a supporting inheritance result, conditional on the assumptions of existing MLP theory, rather than a separate contribution (Appendix~\ref{app:convergence}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\textbf{(1) Geometry-aware recursive correction.} We formulate a common interface for correcting completed value--gradient child estimates before nonlinear reuse, with samplewise metric projection and a shared batch gauge as two realizations.
\textbf{(2) Exact structural rescue.} We give a generator-compatible sufficient condition for pathwise collapse on a published counterexample and derive exact dimension-independent MSE for recursive subspace projection; final-only and generator-only ablations isolate the required insertion points.
\textbf{(3) Mechanism-resolving evidence.} Paired high-dimensional HJB, deeper funding, and gradient-dependent experiments connect final error to generator MSE and the variance of the averaged recursive correction. Thresholded-driver ties, batchwise structural checks, and inactive-constraint controls delimit the finite-budget advantage.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{MLP and gradient-dependent solvers.}
MLP combines Picard iteration, Monte Carlo sampling, and multilevel differences \citep{E2021}. Gradient-dependent variants approximate the solution and its gradient through stochastic representations \citep{HutzenthalerKruse2020,HutzenthalerJentzenKruse2022}. \citet{NeufeldWu2025} develop a scheme for nonconstant coefficients, and \citet{NeufeldNguyenWu2025} establish polynomial complexity under suitable dimension-dependent assumptions. These works supply our numerical engine and conditional convergence theory; we study the feasible-state corrections inserted within that engine.

\paragraph{Truncated MLP: the closest algorithmic precedent.}
\citet{Beck2020} use a priori solution bounds to treat locally Lipschitz coercive scalar reactions, including Allen--Cahn equations. Their truncated driver is $f\circ P_r$, with $P_r$ the interval projector; nonexpansiveness transfers a Lipschitz bound to the modified driver \citep[Eq.~(76), Lemma~3.2]{Beck2020}. Thus PDE-based truncation before nonlinear evaluation and the modified-driver inheritance argument are existing ideas. We extend the numerical focus from scalar reaction truncation to structured value--gradient constraints, including inactive subspaces, joint norm bounds, and normalized sensitivity regions. The substantive questions are which geometry eliminates a harmful gradient-feedback mechanism and how separable versus batch-coupled retractions affect finite-budget error.

\paragraph{Surrogate correction and clipping.}
SCaSML uses a learned PDE surrogate and solves a semilinear defect equation with an MLP corrector at inference time \citep{Fan2026}. Its experimental implementations also clip estimated values and gradients; clipping is therefore not a distinction by itself. Our intervention concerns the geometry of child states passed to the nonlinear driver, rather than the construction or learning of a defect equation. Surrogate correction reduces the defect to be estimated; our retractions control which noisy states the driver reuses. The mechanisms are complementary, and our standalone method requires no trained surrogate.

\paragraph{Hard constraints in learned predictors.}
HardNet embeds hard-constraint enforcement into neural networks and proves universal approximation guarantees for constrained predictors \citep{MinAzizan2025}. We share the use of feasible-set geometry, but apply it to recursive Monte Carlo child estimates rather than trainable network outputs. The relevant guarantees concern recursive numerical error rather than approximation capacity of a learned function.

\paragraph{Constraints on the physical process.}
\citet{Zhong2025} study MLP for drift-control problems with state constraints using reflected dynamics. Those constraints restrict the controlled spatial process. Our sets instead restrict the estimated solution--gradient pair $(u,z)$ at a sampled point, without imposing reflection on the simulated diffusion. The constrained objects, and therefore the numerical modifications, are different.

\paragraph{Dimensional failure and structural rescue.}
\citet{HutzenthalerNguyen2025} identify an HJB family for which the original full-history recursion suffers dimensional deterioration despite a low-dimensional solution. Building on that failure, we identify a sufficient repair condition: every corrected gradient lies in a certified set on which the offending generator vanishes. This yields pathwise collapse for any compatible retraction and an exact MSE for the subspace projector (Theorem~\ref{thm:rescue}). The certificate is supplied by PDE symmetry, not learned from data; after collapse, the estimator equals reduced Monte Carlo. This identifies and repairs an obstruction in the stochastic recurrence while retaining the reduced-dimensional benchmark as the appropriate reference.

\section{Constraint-Aware MLP}
\label{sec:method}
Consider a semilinear parabolic PDE
\begin{equation}
\partial_tu+\mathcal Lu+f(t,x,u,z)=0,\qquad u(T,x)=g(x),
\label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
with gradient-like state $z=\sigma(t,x)^\top\nabla_xu$. MLP recursively estimates $Y=(u,z)$ at random space--time points and reuses those estimates inside $f$. The relevant failure mode is not merely that $\widehat Y$ has large variance; it is that the nonlinear map can destroy cancellation. For example, if the exact gradient obeys $\norm{z}\le 4$ but a finite-sample estimate has norm $12$, then the quadratic HJB driver $f(z)=-\tfrac12\norm z^2$ turns a factor-three state error into a factor-nine driver magnitude. Once this value enters a higher Picard level, a final-output correction is too late. Figure~\ref{fig:interface} shows the insertion point.



\subsection{Two recursive retractions}
Let $Y_1,\ldots,Y_B$ be completed sibling child-state estimates at sampled points $(t_i,x_i)$, with certified sets $\mathcal C_i=\mathcal C(t_i,x_i)$. The formulas suppress the index when all sets coincide.

\paragraph{Samplewise IR.}
For nonempty closed convex $\mathcal C$,
\begin{equation}
\widetilde Y_i=\Pi_{\mathcal C}Y_i.
\label{eq:samplewise}
\end{equation}
This is local, introduces no cross-sample coupling, and makes the smallest Euclidean correction to each child.

\paragraph{Batch-IR.}
For a gauge set $\mathcal C=\{y:\gamma_{\mathcal C}(y)\le1\}$, define
\begin{equation}
\alpha_B=\min\!\left\{1,\frac{1}{\max_i\gamma_{\mathcal C_i}(Y_i)}\right\},
\qquad \widetilde Y_i=\alpha_BY_i.
\label{eq:batch}
\end{equation}
For a common ball, $\alpha_B=\min\{1,R/\max_i\norm{Y_i}\}$. At distinct sampled points each child uses its own gauge. The gradient-only experiments leave the value component unchanged. A common scale preserves relative directions and magnitudes in the constrained coordinates, but couples siblings and moves their empirical mean.

\subsection{Target Preservation and Local Work}
The key deterministic geometry is the projection inequality: for every feasible $Y^\star\in\mathcal C$,
\begin{equation}
\norm{\Pi_{\mathcal C}Y-Y^\star}^2
\le \norm{Y-Y^\star}^2-\dist(Y,\mathcal C)^2.
\label{eq:pyth}
\end{equation}
Thus samplewise projection removes at least the squared overshoot component of child-state error.

\begin{proposition}[Target preservation]
\label{prop:target}
If $Y^\star(t,x)\in\mathcal C(t,x)$ and the retraction is the identity on feasible states, then constraint-aware evaluation leaves the exact generator unchanged:
\[
f\bigl(t,x,\mathcal R(Y^\star(t,x))\bigr)=f(t,x,Y^\star(t,x)).
\]
Hence the correction stabilizes off-target stochastic states rather than changing the intended PDE solution.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Feasibility gives $\mathcal R(Y^\star)=Y^\star$.
\end{proof}

For samplewise convex projection, this can be strengthened under the standard assumptions used by gradient-dependent MLP theory: defining $\bar f(t,x,y)=f(t,x,\Pi_{\mathcal C(t,x)}y)$ yields an ordinary MLP problem with the same feasible solution, and nonexpansiveness of projection does not increase the state Lipschitz constant. We give the short inheritance argument in Appendix~\ref{app:convergence}.

\paragraph{Work order.}
Projection onto the coordinate subspaces, balls, and diagonal-normalized ellipsoids used below costs $O(d)$ per state. Forming a batch gauge and rescaling $B$ $d$-dimensional states costs $O(Bd)$, the same arithmetic order as reading the batch. When the projected problem satisfies the cited convergence theorem with the same parameter scalings, this local overhead preserves its polynomial complexity exponents. For Batch-IR we claim only unchanged per-update arithmetic order; a sharp cost-to-accuracy theory must account for coupling and is future work.

\section{Where Do the Constraints Come From?}
\label{sec:certificates-main}
The method is useful only when $\mathcal C(t,x)$ is certified independently of the noisy solver output. We use three kinds of structure. First, translation symmetry can force inactive gradient coordinates to be exactly zero, producing a linear subspace. Second, Hopf--Cole or Lipschitz arguments can give norm balls for gradients. Third, financial sensitivities can yield state-dependent ellipsoids in normalized coordinates. These are not thresholds tuned to realized test error.

For the Rosenbrock HJB benchmark, the terminal condition is $g(x)=\log((1+x^\top Ax)/2)$ and $\norm{\nabla g(x)}\le\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A)}$. The Hopf--Cole representation carries this envelope backward; with the benchmark convention $z=\sqrt2\nabla u$ and $\lambda_{\max}(A)\le7.5$, we obtain the non-oracle ball $\norm z\le\sqrt{15}$. In the Neufeld--Wu benchmark, a flow-sensitivity bound places the exact gradient well below the threshold where the driver activates. In the funding problem, the certificate is an ellipsoid in normalized delta coordinates. Derivations are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:certificates}.

\section{Exact Structural Rescue}
\label{sec:rescue}
Consider the family studied by \citet{HutzenthalerNguyen2025}:
\begin{equation}
g_d(x)=|x_1|,\qquad
f_d(v)=\left(\sum_{j=2}^dv_j^2\right)^{1/2},
\label{eq:counterdata}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\partial_tu_d+\tfrac12\Delta u_d+f_d(\nabla u_d)=0,
\qquad u_d(1,x)=g_d(x).
\label{eq:counterpde}
\end{equation}
Although the target depends only on $x_1$, the full-history recursion estimates all $d$ gradient coordinates. Noise in coordinates $2{:}d$ is nonnegative after $f_d$ and becomes a spurious recursive source. The source paper proves that the unprojected method suffers a dimensional lower bound with depth-dependent quantifiers; Appendix~\ref{app:counter} states it precisely.

Translation symmetry certifies the active subspace
\[
S_d=\operatorname{span}(e_1),\qquad \nabla u_d(t,x)\in S_d,
\]
and the driver is exactly compatible with it:
\begin{equation}
f_d(v)=0\quad\text{for all }v\in S_d.
\label{eq:null}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Structural rescue and dimension-free error]
\label{thm:rescue}
Apply any recursive correction before every $f_d$ evaluation such that each corrected child gradient lies in $S_d$ almost surely. Then all nonlinear multilevel corrections vanish pathwise. In particular, for orthogonal projection $Q_d$ onto $S_d$ at every recursive return, including the final gradient, at $(t,x)=(0,0)$ with $N=m^n$ terminal samples,
\begin{equation}
\E\norm{\widehat Y^{\rm IR}_{n,m}-Y^\star}^2
=\frac{4-2/\pi}{N},
\label{eq:exactmse}
\end{equation}
independent of ambient dimension $d$. Any batch-coupled correction of the form $v_i\mapsto\alpha_BQ_dv_i$, $0\le\alpha_B\le1$, has the same pathwise nonlinear collapse.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:null} makes every corrected generator value zero, recursively, so the full nonlinear tree collapses to the projected terminal block. At the origin the only nonzero estimated components are
$N^{-1}\sum_i|G_i|$ and $N^{-1}\sum_i|G_i|G_i$, with $G_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. They are unbiased with variances $(1-2/\pi)/N$ and $3/N$; summing gives~\eqref{eq:exactmse}. The batch statement follows because $\alpha_BQ_dv_i\in S_d$.
\end{proof}

The theorem identifies the essential property: not clipping magnitude, but returning to a \emph{generator-compatible certified set}. Final-only projection cannot undo earlier nonlinear feedback; generator-only projection fixes the value path but leaves inactive gradient noise in the reported state. Exact ablation identities are in Appendix~\ref{app:counter}.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Counterexample: the mechanism is visible}
We reproduce the source recurrence using its random-time law $\mathbb P(r\le b)=\sqrt b$, independent child trees in each multilevel difference, and matched random trees across methods. We test $d\in\{2,5,10,20,50,100,200,500,1000\}$ and $n=m\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ with 512 estimator replicas per configuration. Time and active-coordinate streams are coupled across dimensions, so a flat subspace-IR curve is an expected consequence of the theorem rather than post-hoc smoothing. We separately validate the closed-form MSE with 65,536 fresh Gaussian replicas for each $N\in\{1,4,27,256\}$.

\subsection{Ties, Structural Checks, and Negative Controls}
\paragraph{Thresholded Neufeld--Wu driver.}
At $n=M=2$, 30 runs at each of $d=100,200,300$ give Samplewise IR and Batch-IR identical MAEs of $0.02131$, $0.01682$, and $0.01737$, versus raw errors $0.06066$, $0.03878$, and $0.04039$. Both corrections keep the driver inactive, as in the earlier $n=M=3$ comparison. These are ties, not batchwise wins (Table~\ref{tab:extended-nw}). The implementation retains a finite-step forward approximation, so these are not exact-time convergence measurements.

\paragraph{Batchwise structural sanity check.}
A separate 512-replica study at $n=m=3$ tests $z_i\mapsto\alpha_BQ_dz_i$ for $d=10,100,1000$. Corrected \emph{value} outputs agree realization by realization, with maximum discrepancy zero and value RMSE $0.110083$. The batch scale activates in $58.98\%$ of tested groups. Equality therefore reflects generator compatibility, not an inactive correction. The batch implementation does not project its returned root gradient; this test is not a verification of equal full-state MSE (Appendix~\ref{app:extended-sanity}).

\paragraph{Negative controls and mean preservation.}
The repository's paired Batch-IR controls confirm exact value-output overlap in Allen--Cahn and credit risk when constraints are inactive. In linear convection--diffusion, batch correction activates in 100\%, 97\%, and 8.5\% of recorded groups at the three budgets, yet the value remains unchanged because $f=0$ (Appendix~\ref{app:restored-negative}). The earlier panels and mean-preserving comparisons remain in Figures~\ref{fig:allen-control} and~\ref{fig:batch-tradeoff}. A controlled surrogate-quality study, including cases where IR loses to raw defect MLP, is retained in Appendix~\ref{app:surrogate-diagnostic}; it is not a trained-SCaSML comparison.

\section{What the Experiments Establish}
The counterexample separates a structural claim from a statistical design choice. The structural claim is that recursive infeasibility can create a spurious nonlinear source, and that a generator-compatible certificate can remove it exactly. The statistical choice is how to move noisy samples into the feasible set. Equation~\eqref{eq:pyth} favors nearest-point projection for one-state Euclidean error; Figures~\ref{fig:extended-outcomes} and~\ref{fig:batch-tradeoff} show that a common batch scale can nevertheless be better after nonlinear recursion. This is not a contradiction: two complete recursive schemes generate different later states, and batch coupling changes the mean, dispersion, and cross-sibling dependence. The new diagnostics support lower generator MSE and averaged-correction variance, not uniform reduction of every variance statistic.

A mean-preserving batch diagnostic sharpens the point. When the raw batch mean is feasible, shrinking deviations around that mean can improve final error without changing the empirical mean. When the batch mean itself is outside a convex feasible set, no all-feasible mean-preserving correction exists, because the mean of feasible points must be feasible. Thus low-budget high-dimensional failure can affect the batch center as well as isolated outliers.

\section{Limitations}
The exact rescue uses known PDE structure rather than discovering an active subspace, and its collapsed estimator equals reduced Monte Carlo. Approximate or learned regions need a separate misspecification analysis. Feasibility preserves the intended solution, but does not alone prove consistency or order the final errors of two different recursive solvers. Samplewise inheritance still requires the regularity, moment, norm, and dimension-scaling assumptions of the cited MLP theorem. Batch-IR changes dependence and finite-sample bias; its sharp consistency and cost-to-accuracy theory remain future work. The finance envelope also requires the additional justification noted in Appendix~\ref{app:certificates}. The extension uses 10 HJB repetitions on fixed test sets and matrices, 100 funding estimates against a numerical reference, and finite-step Neufeld--Wu dynamics. These finite-budget comparisons do not prove universal dominance over MLP or trained surrogate solvers.

\section{Conclusion}
MLP can amplify Monte Carlo noise when infeasible intermediate states are passed through nonlinear generators. Constraint-aware MLP inserts a certified retraction exactly at that interface. The strongest result is structural: on a published dimensionality counterexample, enforcing the PDE-certified active subspace removes all nonlinear corrections pathwise and changes the error from ambient-dimension dependent to the exact dimension-free rate $(4-2/\pi)/N$. Beyond that example, paired 100--160D HJB and deeper funding experiments favor Batch-IR, while thresholded-driver ties and inactive-constraint controls delimit the gain. Matched diagnostics isolate lower generator MSE and averaged-correction variance without claiming lower individual-increment variance everywhere. The central contribution is the link between certified value--gradient geometry and the nonlinear feedback it removes, supported by an exact rescue theorem and paired evidence on separable versus batch-coupled corrections.

\section{Samplewise Convergence Inheritance}
\label{app:convergence}
This appendix records a conditional value--gradient version of the modified-driver argument already used for scalar truncated MLP by \citet[Eq.~(76), Lemma~3.2]{Beck2020}. It supplies a correctness check for the specified insertion point, not a new full-history convergence analysis.

For each $(t,x)$ let $\mathcal C(t,x)$ be nonempty, closed, and convex, let $\Pi_{t,x}$ be its metric projector, and define
\begin{equation}
\bar f(t,x,y)=f(t,x,\Pi_{t,x}y).
\label{eq:barf}
\end{equation}
Assume $(t,x,y)\mapsto\Pi_{t,x}y$ is measurable, the exact state $Y^\star(t,x)$ is feasible, $f$ is uniformly $L$-Lipschitz on $\mathcal C(t,x)$ in its state argument, and the remaining coefficient, terminal-data, moment, and random-time assumptions of the chosen gradient-dependent MLP theorem hold for $\bar f$.

\begin{proposition}[Inheritance for pointwise Samplewise IR]
Under these assumptions, $\bar f$ is globally $L$-Lipschitz in $y$, the intended feasible solution also solves the PDE with $f$ replaced by $\bar f$, and the recursion that projects each child only immediately before a driver call is pathwise identical to ordinary MLP applied to $\bar f$ on the same random tree. Hence the corresponding published convergence and complexity bounds apply to Samplewise IR under their remaining assumptions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Metric projection is nonexpansive, so
\begin{align*}
|\bar f(t,x,y)-\bar f(t,x,y')|
&\le L\norm{\Pi_{t,x}y-\Pi_{t,x}y'}\\
&\le L\norm{y-y'}.
\end{align*}
Feasibility gives $\Pi_{t,x}Y^\star=Y^\star$, hence $\bar f(t,x,Y^\star)=f(t,x,Y^\star)$. Finally, at every finite MLP depth, replacing a child $Y$ by $\Pi_{t,x}Y$ before evaluating $f$ produces exactly the call $\bar f(t,x,Y)$. Induction on depth gives pathwise identity with ordinary MLP for $\bar f$, after which the existing theorem applies \citep{HutzenthalerKruse2020,HutzenthalerJentzenKruse2022,NeufeldWu2025,NeufeldNguyenWu2025}.
\end{proof}

This argument does \emph{not} cover Batch-IR because $\alpha_B$ depends jointly on siblings. It also does not cover a different algorithm that first projects samples and then averages them into a new state estimator: for nonlinear $\Pi$, generally $\E[\Pi(Y)]\neq\Pi(\E Y)$. Fixed affine/subspace projections are special because they commute with averaging. A diagonal-normalized ellipsoid uses a weighted metric, not necessarily the Euclidean metric; applying an inheritance theorem additionally requires its norm, regularity, and dimension-dependent bounds to be checked in the same coordinates. Euclidean nonexpansiveness alone does not establish the dimension-uniform coordinate or maximum-norm bounds required by a particular complexity theorem.

\section{Counterexample Details and Ablations}
\label{app:counter}
\subsection{Source lower bound and certification}
Let $U^d_{n,m}$ denote the source full-history recursion and set $Y_d^\star=(u_d,\nabla u_d)$. \citet{HutzenthalerNguyen2025} show that for every $p\ge0$ and every integer $n>2p$,
\[
\liminf_{d\to\infty}d^{-p}
\left(\E\norm{U^d_{n,n}(0,0)-Y_d^\star(0,0)}^2\right)^{1/2}
=\infty.
\]
This is a depth-dependent asymptotic statement, not a claim that one fixed depth grows faster than every polynomial.

For any translation $a$ with $a_1=0$, $u_d(t,x+a)$ has the same PDE and terminal data as $u_d(t,x)$. Uniqueness gives invariance in coordinates $2{:}d$, hence $\partial_{x_j}u_d=0$ for $j\ge2$. The PDE reduces to the heat equation and
\[
u_d(t,x)=\E|x_1+\sqrt{1-t}\,G|,
\qquad G\sim\mathcal N(0,1).
\]

\subsection{Ablations}
If subspace projection is used only inside the generator but not on the returned gradient, nonlinear corrections still vanish, but the inactive final-gradient coordinates remain. At the origin,
\[
\E\norm{\widehat Y_{\rm gen-only}-Y^\star}^2
=\frac{d+3-2/\pi}{N}.
\]
If projection is applied only at the final state, the value component is unchanged realization by realization from raw MLP, because all earlier generator calls are identical.

For the first terminal block,
\begin{align*}
\E\norm{V_{\perp,N}}^2&=\frac{d-1}{N},\qquad
\E f_d(V_N)^2=\frac{d-1}{N},\\
f_d(Q_dV_N)&=0.
\end{align*}
This quantifies the first conversion of centered inactive-gradient noise into a positive nonlinear source.

\section{Certificates Used in the Broader Benchmarks}
\label{app:certificates}
\paragraph{Rosenbrock HJB.}
For $u_t+\Delta u-\norm{\nabla u}^2=0$ and $g(x)=\log((1+x^\top Ax)/2)$,
\[
\nabla g(x)=\frac{2Ax}{1+x^\top Ax},\qquad
\norm{\nabla g(x)}\le\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A)}.
\]
The Hopf--Cole representation carries the same envelope backward in time. With the benchmark convention $z=\sqrt2\nabla u$ and coefficient range giving $\lambda_{\max}(A)\le7.5$, we use $\norm z\le\sqrt{15}$.

\paragraph{Neufeld--Wu.}
The public example has linear drift $\mu(x)=0.06x$, additive diffusion, a 1-Lipschitz terminal payoff, and a thresholded driver proportional to $(\norm z_\infty-25)_+$. Flow sensitivity gives $\norm{\nabla u(t,\cdot)}\le e^{0.06(T-t)}\le e^{0.015}$ over the benchmark horizon, well below the activation threshold.

\paragraph{Nonlinear funding.}
For the 100D borrowing/lending benchmark, a model-derived delta envelope yields
\[
\sum_{i=1}^d\left(\frac{z_i}{\sigma x_i}\right)^2
\le \exp(\sigma^2(T-t)),
\]
which defines a state-dependent ellipsoid in normalized delta coordinates. This is a model-derived envelope; a complete viscosity-solution justification for this particular finance bound remains future work. The implementation performs a Euclidean ball projection after delta normalization, equivalently a weighted-metric correction in the original gradient state.
